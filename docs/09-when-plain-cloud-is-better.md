# Chapter 9 — When Plain Cloud Is the Better Choice

Time: an honest half hour of reflection. Tools: no hardware. This is the most liberating chapter in the book, and the previous eight are what earned it.

A guide that only ever says "host it yourself" is a brochure, not a friend. The truth this book has been building toward: **self-hosting is a trade** — you give away some readiness and availability, and in exchange you keep ownership and pocket money. For plenty of family jobs that trade is wonderful. For some, it is quietly terrible, and the *right* answer is to keep paying Google, Apple, or a modest service — without a gram of guilt.

You now know exactly what you traded, because you built it: the hours in [chapter 3](03-the-first-evening.md), the VPN lane in chapter 4, the care in chapter 5, the discipline of chapter 8. This chapter is the comparison you can finally make fairly.

## What self-hosting actually buys you (and what it costs)

What you gain:

- **Ownership.** The photos and files sit on your drive, under your rules, subject to no policy change, price hike, or "we have sunset this feature" announcement.
- **Privacy, structurally.** The private lane means strangers' systems do not see the data as products in the making.
- **Money.** After hardware you likely already own: electricity for the season, less than a coffee a month.
- **Skills, transferable and real.** Worth saying plainly: everything learned here is genuinely useful professional-adjacent competence.

What you pay:

- **Responsibility.** Updates, the seasonal restore drill, the day something breaks and the family needs it working twelve minutes before dinner.
- **The sharp edge of one household.** Your home internet, power, and attention span are the infrastructure. A cloud provider's worst day is a busy warehouse somewhere; your worst day is upstairs.
- **The odd awkwardness.** The iPhone background-upload dance from [chapter 5](05-file-and-photo-sync.md), the "the wifi is down so the library is too" moment.

Both lists are real. Here is where each side wins.

## Where the cloud plainly wins

These are not consolation answers; they are correct answers, and any veteran of home servers endorsed all of them:

- **Email, always.** [Chapter 1's red list](01-what-is-safe-to-host.md#red-do-not-host-from-home-or-only-with-serious-caution) said it; here it is again with feeling: a reputable provider, done. Self-hosted email is a specialist's career that punishes beginners with years of lost replies.
- **Anything that must never be unavailable.** If a service's being down is a five-alarm problem — a small-business order list, a family member's critical reminders — the cloud's fleet of buildings is worth its fee. Home servers are for jobs where down-until-7pm is acceptable.
- **Photos where iPhone households live.** [Chapter 5](05-file-and-photo-sync.md#the-phone-question-answered-plainly) told the truth: Apple's ecosystem makes background photo life frictionless on *their* service and polite-but-awkward on yours. A mainstream cloud library as the *first* home of iPhone photos, with the family server as second — or as the home of the Android half — is a stable, honest arrangement. So is cloud-first for the whole camera roll. The photos' safety outranks the ideology, every time.
- **When the "users" would rather not.** A family wiki survives on five minutes of goodwill per month. If the goodwill is not there — if the family quietly drifts back to whatever they were using — the wiki will not out-love the app they already open. Chapter 6's habits are the honest attempt; chapter 9's honest exit is the fallback.
- **Video calls, streaming subscriptions, big shared calendars** — collaborative spaces that thrive on other people's uptime and integration, moved here for you by millions of users who are not wrong.
- **Backup to a good cloud tier as the offsite copy.** [Chapter 8](08-backups.md) already made the case in your favor: a modest cloud plan holding the irreplaceable subset is legitimate 3-2-1 compliance, not a concession.

## The hybrid: the answer almost everyone lands on

Almost nobody's good setup is 100% cloud or 100% self-hosted — the trade is worth making *per job*, and here is the shape the honest trackers converge on:

| The job | The calm winner | Why it wins |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family files, wiki, home movies | **Home server** | Ownership, privacy, free forever |
| The camera roll | **Either, or both** | Server for Android households; cloud for iPhone-heavy ones; honesty beats ideology |
| Email | **Cloud, always** | See above, with feeling |
| A public website | **Free static host** | Chapter 7's honest bridge — zero uptime anxiety |
| The irreplaceable subset's second copy | **Cloud tier (paid, modest)** | Chapter 8's offsite "1," insured and done |
| Anything the business runs on | **Cloud** | Uptime you did not earn, midnight you did not spend |
| The bulk of +1 TB of media and files | **Home server, with cloud shelf for irreplaceables** | The cloud bill for *everything* is the thing nobody re-checks; the cloud bill for *meaningful* things is a bargain |

Read that table as a lifestyle, not a compromise: the household keeps its *ownership* of the big, personal, joyful data; the household rents *reliability* only where reliability is worth a bill.

## A guilt-free decision test for the next "should I host it?" idea

Ask — out loud, with chapter 1's traffic light in one hand and this chapter in the other:

1. **Is it green-zone?** If it cannot be (email, strangers' data, must-never-be-down), the cloud wins its place on the team.
2. **Does my household *have* the evening for it?** One service per evening is the pace; the queue has to fit real weeks.
3. **Will this job survive my worst Tuesday?** If the job needs a service level your household cannot guarantee, the cloud is the teammate, not the rival.
4. **What does each option cost per year, truly?** Money, hours, worry — cheap versions of all three exist on both sides; pick by totals, not loyalty.

If the honest answer at any of the four is "cloud," pick cloud. *The goal was never 100% self-hosted; the goal is a family's digital life that is owned where ownership is valuable and reliable where reliability is critical.* Both at once. That is the good life, and it fits on one shelf.
